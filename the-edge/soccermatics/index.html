Soccermatics

Why football's chaos is exactly what makes it mathematically interesting.

by David Sumpter · 2016

Share on X

Sumpter, a mathematician, applies pattern-finding tools — game theory, network analysis, chaos theory — to football, arguing that the sport's fluid unpredictability is exactly what makes mathematics useful for understanding it, not what makes math irrelevant.

The book moves from passing networks and Barcelona's tiki-taka to crowd behavior and prediction markets, always grounding abstract concepts in specific matches and players rather than staying in the abstract.

It's one of the most accessible entry points into football analytics precisely because Sumpter resists jargon and builds intuition before formality — the tradeoff being that readers already fluent in xG and network graphs may find the first half a review.

Sumpter's chapter on passing networks is the clearest demonstration of his method — he shows how treating a team's passing as a mathematical network reveals structural patterns (who a team actually depends on to connect play, not just who touches the ball most) that are invisible from raw pass-completion stats, which is the kind of insight that only becomes visible once you stop counting and start modeling relationships.

Key ideas

  • Football's fluid unpredictability is exactly what makes mathematics useful for understanding it, not what makes math irrelevant.
  • The player a team depends on most isn't always the one with the most touches — network math finds who actually holds it together.
  • The book moves from passing networks and Barcelona's tiki-taka to crowd behavior and prediction markets, always grounded in specific matches.
  • It's one of the most accessible entry points into football analytics precisely because Sumpter builds intuition before formality.
Worth it if

you want a single well-written book that explains why data and mathematics belong in football analysis, pitched at intelligent general readers rather than working analysts.
