The Not-to-Do List

The Surprisingly Simple Art of Success

by Rolf Dobelli · 2025

Share on X

Dobelli's premise, following his earlier work on cognitive bias, is that success is driven less by what high performers do than by what they systematically avoid — bad decisions, wasted effort, and predictable failure patterns that most success literature ignores in favor of positive habits to copy.

The book is structured as a set of short, standalone "not-to-do" principles rather than a single argument, each built around a specific failure mode drawn from psychology, business case studies, and Dobelli's own investing and writing career.

The format trades depth for breadth in the same way his other books do — each principle gets a few pages, which makes it an easy, browsable read but means few ideas get the sustained treatment a harder case might need to be fully convincing.

The most memorable entries aren't the abstract ones but the specific failure patterns he names precisely — the sunk-cost trap, the halo effect, the tendency to mistake activity for progress — because naming a mistake precisely is most of what it takes to catch yourself making it. Dobelli's own admission that he still falls for several of these despite writing about them for years is oddly the most convincing part of the book.

Key ideas

  • Success is driven less by what high performers do than by what they systematically avoid.
  • The fastest way to improve your decisions is usually to stop making a specific mistake, not to add a new habit.
  • Naming a mistake precisely — the sunk-cost trap, the halo effect, mistaking activity for progress — is most of what it takes to catch yourself making it.
  • The short, standalone format trades depth for breadth, so few ideas get sustained treatment, but it makes for an easy, browsable read.
Worth it if

you liked bias-avoidance books and want the same approach applied specifically to career and life success rather than reasoning errors in general.
