Think Twice

A field guide to the decision traps that catch smart, experienced people.

by Michael J. Mauboussin · 2009

Mauboussin, a longtime investment strategist, catalogues the recurring decision-making traps that snare smart, experienced people specifically — overconfidence from past success, mistaking correlation for causation, anchoring on the wrong reference class — and offers concrete, checklist-style habits to counteract each one.

It's shorter and more applied than the denser behavioral-economics literature, aimed at practitioners who need usable tools rather than a full theory of cognition.

Because it's structured as a series of distinct traps, it can read a little like a checklist rather than a flowing argument, and Mauboussin's finance background means many examples lean toward investing — though they translate reasonably well to betting and forecasting.

Mauboussin's chapter on the 'inside view versus outside view' is the one with the widest application beyond investing — he shows that people systematically underrate how useful it is to ask how similar situations turned out for other people instead of reasoning purely from the specifics of their own case, a habit that's directly transferable to betting, forecasting, and any decision made under real uncertainty.

Key ideas

  • Mauboussin catalogues the recurring decision-making traps that snare smart, experienced people specifically — overconfidence from past success, mistaking correlation for causation.
  • Asking how similar situations turned out for other people beats reasoning purely from your own case — and almost nobody does it by default.
  • The "outside view" — how did similar situations turn out for other people — is systematically underused compared to reasoning from your own specifics.
  • It's shorter and more applied than a book like Thinking, Fast and Slow, aimed at practitioners who need usable tools rather than a full theory of cognition.
Worth it if

you want a fast, practical companion to the more theoretical books on this shelf — something to reference before a big decision rather than read once and shelve.
