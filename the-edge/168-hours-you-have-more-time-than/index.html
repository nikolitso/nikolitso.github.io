168 Hours

You have more time than you think — the case, with the math to prove it.

by Laura Vanderkam · 2010

Share on X

Laura Vanderkam’s core move is simple and genuinely useful: everyone has the same 168 hours in a week, so “I don’t have time” claims usually don’t survive an honest, hour-by-hour audit of where the time actually goes. Most people who complain about time scarcity are, once they actually track a week, surprised by how much of it disappears into low-value defaults rather than being genuinely, unavoidably spoken for.

The book’s real contribution isn’t a new productivity system — it’s the audit itself, and the reframe that follows: time management is less about squeezing more into a day and more about being honest about what you’re actually choosing to prioritize versus what you’re just letting happen by default.

The audit itself is the intervention — Vanderkam finds that most people's estimate of how many hours they work or how little free time they have is off by a wide margin in a predictable direction, and simply tracking a real week (not a typical one, an actual one) tends to do more to change behavior than any time-management technique layered on top of a false starting assumption.

Key ideas

  • Everyone has the same 168 hours in a week, so "I don't have time" claims usually don't survive an honest, hour-by-hour audit.
  • Most people's sense of how busy they are is wrong in a specific, measurable way — tracking one real week usually corrects it faster than any productivity system.
  • Most people who complain about time scarcity are surprised, once they actually track a week, by how much disappears into low-value defaults.
  • Time management is less about squeezing more into a day and more about being honest about what you're choosing to prioritize.
Worth it if

“I don’t have time for that” is a phrase you say often, and you’re willing to actually test whether it’s true.
