The Art of Thinking Clearly

99 thinking errors, cataloged so you can catch yourself making them.

by Rolf Dobelli · 2013

Rolf Dobelli spent years collecting the ways smart people reliably think badly — confirmation bias, sunk cost, survivorship bias — and turned the list into what amounts to a field guide to your own mind's shortcuts.

The book is organized as roughly a hundred short chapters, each pinned to one specific error in reasoning, drawn from behavioral economics research (Kahneman and Tversky loom large) but written for a general reader rather than an academic one. The premise is modest and effective: you won't stop being irrational, but you can learn to notice it happening in real time.

Because each chapter stands alone, the book has no real argument beyond "here is another way your brain lies to you" — there's no unifying thesis, and a few entries feel repetitive read back to back. It's built for dipping in and out, not for a single sitting.

Dobelli is at his best when a bias shows up somewhere the reader wouldn't expect it — survivorship bias explaining why 'successful people all did X' advice is nearly always worthless (you never hear from the people who did X and failed), or the sunk-cost fallacy explaining why organizations keep funding failing projects specifically because they've already spent so much on them. The specificity of the examples is what makes a hundred short chapters feel additive rather than repetitive.

Key ideas

  • Dobelli collected the ways smart people reliably think badly — confirmation bias, sunk cost, survivorship bias — into roughly a hundred short chapters.
  • Most bad decisions aren't a failure of intelligence — they're a specific, nameable bias you could have caught if you knew to look for it.
  • Survivorship bias explains why "successful people all did X" advice is nearly always worthless — you never hear from the people who did X and failed.
  • Because each chapter stands alone, there's no unifying thesis beyond "here is another way your brain lies to you" — built for dipping in and out.
Worth it if

you want a working checklist of cognitive traps rather than a deep theoretical treatment of why they exist.
