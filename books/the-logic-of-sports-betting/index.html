The Logic Of Sports Betting

A first-principles case for treating betting as pricing, not picking.

by Ed Miller, Matthew Davidow · 2019

Share on X

Miller is a genuinely credible gambling-strategy author — well-regarded in poker circles for The Course — and here he and Davidow focus less on picks and more on how sportsbooks actually set and move their lines in the first place.

Their core argument: only a few “originating” books employ world-class handicappers to set opening lines, and everyone else largely copies them — creating a brief window of exploitable weakness in early lines, plus a second soft spot in derivative markets like halves, quarters, and player props that are mathematically derived from the main line rather than independently priced.

Notably, the authors deliberately refuse to hand out specific systems or picks, reasoning that anything that reliably worked would get arbitraged away the moment it was published — a reviewer at Philo on Books called it “good, not great,” a solid conceptual framework rather than a source of secrets.

The distinction between originating and derivative markets is the book's most actionable idea — a handful of syndicate-facing sportsbooks pay for genuine expertise to build their main lines, and everything downstream (player props, quarter lines, alternate spreads at other books) is largely derived from that number mathematically, meaning the real informational edge, if one exists at all, is more likely to be found in the derivative markets than by trying to out-handicap the sharpest original line.

Key ideas

  • Only a few "originating" books employ world-class handicappers to set opening lines — everyone else largely copies them.
  • You're rarely smarter than the line that was set first — the real opportunities are in the markets built on top of it.
  • A second soft spot exists in derivative markets like halves, quarters, and player props, which are mathematically derived rather than independently priced.
  • The authors deliberately refuse to hand out specific systems or picks, reasoning that anything that reliably worked would get arbitraged away the moment it was published.
Worth it if

you want to understand market structure and where inefficiencies actually come from, rather than looking for someone else's picks.
