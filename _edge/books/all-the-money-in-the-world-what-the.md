<p>Likely Laura Vanderkam's book on the relationship between money and happiness — her research-driven, unsentimental style applied to a question most personal-finance writing avoids: not how to get more money, but what money actually does and doesn't buy in terms of genuine satisfaction, and where people consistently misjudge that relationship.</p>

<p>The useful version of this argument isn’t “money doesn’t matter” (it clearly does, up to a point) — it’s more specific than that: which purchases and money decisions reliably move happiness, and which ones people assume will and don’t.</p>

<p>The more specific and more useful version of the money-happiness question, per Vanderkam's approach, is comparing categories of spending directly — experiences versus possessions, time-saving purchases versus status purchases — rather than asking the vague question of whether money buys happiness at all, since the research is much clearer on some categories (yes, paying someone to do a chore you hate) than others (no, a bigger house rarely delivers what people expect from it).</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Money clearly matters up to a point, but most personal-finance writing avoids the harder question of what it actually does and doesn't buy in satisfaction.</li><li>The question isn't whether money buys happiness — it's which specific purchases actually do, and most people are buying the wrong ones.</li><li>Paying someone to do a chore you hate reliably moves happiness; a bigger house rarely delivers what people expect from it.</li><li>Comparing categories of spending directly — experiences versus possessions, time-saving versus status — is more useful than asking the vague question.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want a clear-eyed, research-backed look at the money-happiness relationship instead of either “money buys happiness” or “money doesn’t matter” as an unexamined slogan.</p>
