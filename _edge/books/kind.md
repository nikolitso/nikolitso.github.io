<p><strong>Graham Allcott</strong> is the bestselling author of How to Be a Productivity Ninja and the founder of Think Productive, one of the world's leading productivity training companies.</p>

<p>In KIND, Allcott makes the case that kindness at work isn't a soft, nice-to-have trait but a genuine driver of performance, trust, and retention. Drawing on research from psychology and organizational behavior alongside his own experience building and leading teams, he argues that kind leadership — clear, honest, generous with feedback and grace — consistently outperforms the command-and-control style many managers default to under pressure. The book works through what kindness actually looks like in practice: how to give difficult feedback kindly, how to set boundaries without being harsh, and how to build a culture where people do their best work because they feel genuinely supported.</p>

<p>A practical, well-argued case for treating kindness as a management discipline rather than a personality trait.</p>

<p>Allcott is careful to separate kindness from conflict-avoidance — the book's most useful chapters are specifically about delivering hard feedback kindly rather than softening it into vagueness, which he argues is where most well-intentioned managers actually go wrong. The distinction between being nice (avoiding discomfort) and being kind (telling the truth with care) does a lot of work throughout the book.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Kindness at work is a genuine driver of performance, trust, and retention, not just a soft, nice-to-have trait.</li><li>Kind management means telling people the truth, not protecting them from it.</li><li>Kindness is distinct from conflict-avoidance — the useful chapters are about delivering hard feedback kindly rather than softening it into vagueness.</li><li>Kind leadership consistently outperforms the command-and-control style many managers default to under pressure.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want a management case for kindness backed by research and practice, rather than another leadership platitude about being nice.</p>
