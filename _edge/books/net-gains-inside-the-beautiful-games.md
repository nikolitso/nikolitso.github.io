<p>O'Hanlon, a football journalist, traces how data analytics moved from a fringe curiosity to a central part of how top clubs recruit, coach, and play, told through the people and ideas that drove the shift rather than through the math itself.</p>

<p>It's structured as narrative journalism — profiles of analysts, executives, and the clubs that bet early on data — rather than a technical text.</p>

<p>Readers hoping for a hands-on introduction to building models themselves will find this book tells the story around the numbers rather than teaching the numbers themselves.</p>

<p>O'Hanlon is good at showing how the same data-driven idea landed very differently depending on the club culture it entered — a model that got embraced at one club as a genuine edge got quietly shelved at another because the coaching staff never bought in, which is really a story about organizational change as much as football analytics specifically.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Data analytics moved from a fringe curiosity to a central part of how top clubs recruit, coach, and play.</li><li>The same data can be a competitive edge at one club and a filed-away report at another, depending entirely on whether anyone with power believed in it.</li><li>It's structured as narrative journalism — profiles of analysts, executives, and early-adopting clubs — rather than a technical text.</li><li>Readers hoping for a hands-on modeling introduction should look to Soccermatics or Soccer Analytics instead; this tells the story around the numbers.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want the human and institutional story behind football's data revolution, especially alongside a more technical book for the mechanics.</p>
