<p>Man vs Big Data looks at how data and algorithms are reshaping decisions once made on pure human judgment — from sports and dating to hiring and criminal justice — and where that shift genuinely improves outcomes versus where it introduces new, less visible biases.</p>

<p>The strongest material is in the case studies where algorithmic decision-making produced worse or more biased outcomes than the human judgment it replaced, a useful counterweight to the more triumphalist 'data beats intuition' narrative common in this genre.</p>

<p>It's a survey across several domains rather than a deep dive into one, which gives it breadth at some cost to depth in any single area.</p>

<p>The book's most useful service is refusing to let 'algorithmic' and 'objective' collapse into the same thing — Cowley's case studies show algorithms trained on biased historical data reproducing and sometimes amplifying exactly the human biases they were supposed to replace, which is a more specific and more useful critique than a general suspicion of data-driven decision-making.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Data and algorithms are reshaping decisions once made on pure human judgment — from sports and dating to hiring and criminal justice.</li><li>An algorithm trained on biased history doesn't remove the bias — it just makes it harder to see.</li><li>The strongest material shows algorithmic decision-making producing worse or more biased outcomes than the human judgment it replaced.</li><li>It's a survey across several domains rather than a deep dive into one, trading depth in any single area for breadth.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want a balanced look at where algorithmic decision-making helps and where it quietly introduces new problems.</p>
