<p>Most workplace-relationship advice is either HR-department-generic (“communicate openly!”) or purely tactical (how to run a meeting). This one stays closer to the imprint’s actual strength: the psychological undercurrents that make some colleague relationships easy and others quietly draining, and why “just communicate better” is true but not very actionable on its own.</p>

<p>The School of Life's real contribution is naming the specific psychological patterns that make a colleague relationship curdle — projecting a difficult parent onto a difficult boss, reading neutral feedback as a personal verdict — patterns that generic workplace-communication advice never touches because it assumes rational actors rather than people carrying their whole emotional history into a Monday standup.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>"Just communicate better" is true but not very actionable on its own — the real issue is usually the psychological undercurrent beneath a strained relationship.</li><li>Most difficult colleagues aren't a communication problem — they're an old pattern wearing a new face.</li><li>A colleague's overreaction to feedback often has nothing to do with the current situation and everything to do with an old emotional script.</li><li>It narrows in specifically on peer-to-peer relationships, which make or break how bearable a job actually is day to day.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> a specific colleague relationship has been harder than it should be, and you want language for what’s actually going on rather than generic collaboration tips.</p>
