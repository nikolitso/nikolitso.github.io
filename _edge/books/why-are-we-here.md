<p>Moss, a workplace culture researcher, examines the modern crisis of meaning at work — burnout, disengagement, quiet quitting — and argues that purpose isn't a perk companies can bolt on, but something that has to be built into how work is actually structured and led.</p>

<p>She draws on organizational research and interviews to distinguish genuine purpose-building from the superficial "purpose-washing" many companies practice instead.</p>

<p>Like much workplace-culture writing, some prescriptions work more easily at large organizations with dedicated HR resources than at small teams, and the line between actionable guidance and general observation isn't always sharp.</p>

<p>Moss is sharp on the mechanics of purpose-washing specifically — mission statements and values posters that exist alongside working conditions that contradict them, and why employees generally see through the gap faster than leadership expects. The more useful chapters focus on structural fixes (how work gets assigned, how autonomy is actually distributed) rather than messaging fixes, which is where most companies default first.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Purpose isn't a perk companies can bolt on — it has to be built into how work is actually structured and led.</li><li>Purpose isn't something you communicate to employees — it's something they experience in how the work is actually organized.</li><li>Employees generally see through "purpose-washing" — mission statements alongside contradictory working conditions — faster than leadership expects.</li><li>Structural fixes (how work gets assigned, how autonomy is distributed) work better than messaging fixes, which is where most companies default first.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you're building or leading a team and want a research-grounded look at why "purpose" initiatives so often fail to move the needle, and what would actually work instead.</p>
