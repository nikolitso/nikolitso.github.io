<p>Rolf Dobelli spent years collecting the ways smart people reliably think badly — confirmation bias, sunk cost, survivorship bias — and turned the list into what amounts to a field guide to your own mind's shortcuts.</p>

<p>The book is organized as roughly a hundred short chapters, each pinned to one specific error in reasoning, drawn from behavioral economics research (Kahneman and Tversky loom large) but written for a general reader rather than an academic one. The premise is modest and effective: you won't stop being irrational, but you can learn to notice it happening in real time.</p>

<p>Because each chapter stands alone, the book has no real argument beyond "here is another way your brain lies to you" — there's no unifying thesis, and a few entries feel repetitive read back to back. It's built for dipping in and out, not for a single sitting.</p>

<p>Dobelli is at his best when a bias shows up somewhere the reader wouldn't expect it — survivorship bias explaining why 'successful people all did X' advice is nearly always worthless (you never hear from the people who did X and failed), or the sunk-cost fallacy explaining why organizations keep funding failing projects specifically because they've already spent so much on them. The specificity of the examples is what makes a hundred short chapters feel additive rather than repetitive.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Dobelli collected the ways smart people reliably think badly — confirmation bias, sunk cost, survivorship bias — into roughly a hundred short chapters.</li><li>Most bad decisions aren't a failure of intelligence — they're a specific, nameable bias you could have caught if you knew to look for it.</li><li>Survivorship bias explains why "successful people all did X" advice is nearly always worthless — you never hear from the people who did X and failed.</li><li>Because each chapter stands alone, there's no unifying thesis beyond "here is another way your brain lies to you" — built for dipping in and out.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want a working checklist of cognitive traps rather than a deep theoretical treatment of why they exist.</p>
