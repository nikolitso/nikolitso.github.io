<p>This book takes on a counterintuitive idea: that limitations — of time, resources, information, or freedom — often produce better thinking and better outcomes than having unlimited options, because constraints force real prioritization instead of endless optimization.</p>

<p>It draws on research across psychology, design, and decision-making to show how removing choices can sharpen judgment rather than merely restrict it, echoing themes familiar from other behavioral-science books on this shelf.</p>

<p>As with much of this genre, the argument occasionally outruns the evidence in its more sweeping claims, and some case studies work better as illustration than as proof; it's most convincing when it stays close to specific, well-documented examples.</p>

<p>Epstein is careful to distinguish productive constraints from arbitrary ones — a strict deadline that forces prioritization is different from a bureaucratic rule that just adds friction — and the book spends real time on how to design the former rather than stumble into the latter. The most useful chapters treat constraint-setting as a skill in its own right, not just a fact of life to be endured.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Limitations of time, resources, or freedom often produce better thinking than unlimited options, because constraints force real prioritization instead of endless optimization.</li><li>The right constraint doesn't limit your options so much as it tells you which ones actually matter.</li><li>Productive constraints are designed, not just endured — a strict deadline that forces prioritization is different from a bureaucratic rule that just adds friction.</li><li>The book is most convincing when it stays close to specific, well-documented case studies rather than its more sweeping claims.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want a genuinely different angle on decision-making — an argument for the discipline constraints impose, rather than another book about avoiding bias.</p>
