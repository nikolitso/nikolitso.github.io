<p>Manteris ran sportsbooks in Las Vegas for decades, and this book is his insider account of the job — how odds actually get set and moved, how bookmakers manage risk against sharp bettors and public money alike, and the pressure of running a book during major events.</p>

<p>It's memoir as much as instruction, told from the side of the business that most betting books never show directly.</p>

<p>Because it's written from decades of specific Vegas experience, some of the operational detail is dated relative to today's largely online, algorithm-driven sportsbook industry, so treat it as a window into how the trade traditionally worked rather than a current operations manual.</p>

<p>Manteris is at his best describing the adversarial relationship between the book and its sharpest customers — the constant, largely invisible negotiation over where a line should sit, and how quickly a book has to react when professional money starts moving a number. It's a useful corrective for anyone who thinks of odds as a fixed statement of probability rather than a live, defensive instrument.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>An odds line isn't a prediction — it's a moving target the book is constantly defending.</li><li>Bookmakers manage risk against both sharp bettors and public money simultaneously, not just set a number and wait.</li><li>The constant, largely invisible negotiation over where a line should sit is the real skill of running a book.</li><li>Because it's grounded in decades of Vegas-specific experience, treat it as a window into how the trade traditionally worked rather than a current operations manual.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want to understand betting from the house's side of the counter — useful context for any bettor trying to understand why lines move the way they do.</p>
