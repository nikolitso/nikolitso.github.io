<p>Another School of Life title, A Job to Love pushes back on the modern expectation that work should be a primary source of meaning and identity, arguing that this expectation — relatively new historically — sets most people up for disappointment, and that a more honest relationship with work starts with adjusting that expectation.</p>

<p>The most useful reframe is separating 'a job you love' from 'a job that's good enough and leaves room for meaning elsewhere' — the book argues the second is both more achievable and, counterintuitively, often more satisfying long-term than chasing the first.</p>

<p>As with the rest of the series, it's a short philosophical essay rather than a career-planning workbook — don't expect resume advice, expect a reframe of what you're even optimizing for.</p>

<p>The book traces this expectation historically — the idea that work should be a calling rather than just a livelihood is a relatively recent, largely post-industrial invention, and The School of Life's case is that treating it as timeless wisdom rather than a modern cultural assumption is exactly what makes it so quietly damaging when a job inevitably falls short of it.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>The expectation that work should be a primary source of meaning and identity is relatively new historically, and sets most people up for disappointment.</li><li>Expecting work to supply your sense of meaning is a modern invention, not a timeless truth — and it sets most jobs up to disappoint.</li><li>Separating "a job you love" from "a job that's good enough and leaves room for meaning elsewhere" is more achievable and often more satisfying long-term.</li><li>It's a short philosophical essay rather than a career-planning workbook — expect a reframe of what you're optimizing for, not resume advice.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you're questioning whether your job needs to be your passion, and want a philosophical rather than tactical take on that question.</p>
