<p>Buchdahl's focus here isn't betting mechanics at all — it's psychology: why a small group of bettors (“sharps”) consistently win over time while the much larger group (“squares”) consistently don't, even when both have access to the same odds.</p>

<p>He explicitly interrogates whether betting markets can be reliably beaten at all, and whether the tipster-service industry built around “expert picks” actually has any real value — a genuinely skeptical, myth-puncturing angle for a book in this genre.</p>

<p>Traditionally published through Oldcastle Books rather than self-published, and Buchdahl's ongoing work running Football-Data.co.uk gives him real standing in the space — one of the more credentialed entries in this whole batch.</p>

<p>Buchdahl's skepticism toward the tipster industry is the book's sharpest material — he walks through why a service can post a genuinely impressive win rate over a cherry-picked sample while still being worthless or worse for a subscriber following it in real time, a distinction most marketing around paid tips is specifically designed to obscure.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>A small group of bettors ("sharps") consistently win over time while a much larger group ("squares") consistently don't, even with access to the same odds.</li><li>A tipster's past win rate tells you almost nothing about whether following them going forward is a good bet.</li><li>Buchdahl explicitly interrogates whether betting markets can be reliably beaten at all — a genuinely skeptical, myth-puncturing angle for the genre.</li><li>Buchdahl's ongoing work running Football-Data.co.uk gives him real standing in the space, among the more credentialed entries on this shelf.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want the psychological “why do sharps win” answer before diving into any specific technical betting method.</p>
