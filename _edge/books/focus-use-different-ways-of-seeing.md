<p>This is very likely Daniel Goleman’s Focus — his follow-up to Emotional Intelligence, arguing that attention itself is a trainable skill underlying almost everything else people call talent or leadership, from creativity to self-control to reading a room. (You had this listed as an audiobook, which fits — it’s the kind of book that works well narrated.)</p>

<p>Goleman’s case draws on cognitive science to split attention into a few different modes — inward (self-awareness), outward (reading other people and situations), and other-focused (understanding what someone else needs) — and argues that most advice about “focus” only ever trains one of the three, usually the least socially useful one.</p>

<p>It’s a slightly less famous entry than Emotional Intelligence, but the central idea — that attention is a skill you can deliberately build, not a fixed trait — is a useful complement to the noise-and-bias arguments elsewhere on this shelf; Kahneman explains why judgment goes wrong, Goleman is closer to a practical answer for training the attention that judgment depends on.</p>

<p>The three-way split between inner, outer, and other focus is the part that travels best into a management or team context — Goleman's case is that most professional advice trains only the inward mode (self-discipline, willpower) while systematically neglecting the outward and other-focused modes that are what actually let someone read a room or anticipate what a colleague needs before being asked.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Attention itself is a trainable skill underlying almost everything people call talent or leadership.</li><li>Most advice about focus only trains you to manage yourself — not to read the people and situations around you.</li><li>Attention splits into inward (self-awareness), outward (reading situations), and other-focused (understanding what someone else needs) modes.</li><li>Most professional advice trains only the inward mode while neglecting the outward and other-focused modes that let someone read a room.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you’re interested in the mechanics of attention as a skill, not just the psychology of why we get decisions wrong.</p>
