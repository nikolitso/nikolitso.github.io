<p>Drawing on Tetlock's Good Judgment Project, this book identifies what separates people who are actually good at predicting future events from the much larger group who merely sound confident — traits like updating beliefs incrementally, thinking in probabilities rather than certainties, and actively seeking disconfirming evidence.</p>

<p>It's grounded in real tournament data rather than intuition about what good forecasting should look like, with case studies of individual "superforecasters" keeping the abstract material concrete.</p>

<p>The central findings are compelling, but the book occasionally repeats its core points across chapters in slightly different framings, and readers hoping for a step-by-step system will find more philosophy than checklist.</p>

<p>The book's most counterintuitive finding is that domain expertise correlates surprisingly weakly with forecasting accuracy — some of Tetlock's best performers were serious hobbyists rather than credentialed specialists, and what predicted accuracy instead was a specific cognitive style: breaking a big question into smaller measurable pieces, updating in small increments rather than dramatic swings, and treating a forecast as a probability to keep revising rather than a fixed prediction to defend.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>What separates people who are actually good at predicting future events from those who merely sound confident: updating beliefs incrementally, thinking in probabilities, seeking disconfirming evidence.</li><li>Being a good forecaster has less to do with what you know than with how willing you are to keep updating what you think.</li><li>Domain expertise correlates surprisingly weakly with forecasting accuracy — some of the best performers were serious hobbyists, not credentialed specialists.</li><li>It's grounded in real tournament data from the Good Judgment Project rather than intuition about what good forecasting should look like.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want an evidence-based answer to what actually makes predictions reliable — directly relevant to betting, business forecasting, or any domain where you're pricing uncertainty.</p>
