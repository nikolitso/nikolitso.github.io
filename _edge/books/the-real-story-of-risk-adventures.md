<p>Croston, a biologist by training, traces human risk-misjudgment back to evolutionary mismatch — instincts built for ancient survival threats that misfire badly on the specific shape of modern risks.</p>

<p>The clearest example he uses: people fear rare, vivid dangers like shark attacks and plane crashes far more than common, slow-moving ones like heart disease, which actually causes roughly one in five deaths — because vivid narrative overrides statistical reality in how humans actually process danger, whether the topic is 401k allocation, food safety, or climate change.</p>

<p>It's general-audience risk psychology rather than sports or betting-specific, so the direct application to betting decisions is something you bring yourself — but the underlying lesson (your gut's sense of “risky” and the actual statistics frequently disagree) is exactly the trap every bettor on this shelf needs to watch for.</p>

<p>Croston's evolutionary framing does real explanatory work beyond the shark-attack example — he traces the specific ancestral logic behind why humans overweight vivid, sudden, agent-caused threats (an attack, a crash) over slow, statistical, cause-diffuse ones (diet, chronic disease, gradual financial erosion), because the first category was what actually threatened survival for most of human history and the second simply didn't exist in a form evolution had time to build instincts around.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Human risk-misjudgment traces back to evolutionary mismatch — instincts built for ancient survival threats misfire badly on modern risks.</li><li>Your instincts are calibrated for ancient dangers, not the statistical shape of modern risk — which is exactly why they keep getting modern risk wrong.</li><li>People fear rare, vivid dangers like shark attacks far more than common, slow-moving ones like heart disease, which actually causes roughly one in five deaths.</li><li>Vivid narrative overrides statistical reality in how humans process danger, whether the topic is investing, food safety, or climate change.</li></ul>

<p><strong>Worth it if:</strong> you want the evolutionary-psychology explanation for why your gut is such an unreliable narrator about actual risk.</p>
