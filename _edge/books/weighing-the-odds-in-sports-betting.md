<p>Yao treats sportsbooks the way a trader treats a market — the whole book is built around expected value as the one concept that actually matters, with everything else in service of estimating it accurately.</p>

<p>He frames sharp bettors explicitly as “traders” exploiting pricing inefficiencies, recommends conservative bankroll rules (1–2% of bankroll per wager), and specifically warns against progressive betting systems like the Martingale that feel intuitively appealing but are mathematically dangerous.</p>

<p>Published in 2007, some of the specific market-structure advice — how much line-shopping value is actually available, how books behave — is dated relative to today's more efficient, algorithmically-priced betting markets, though the underlying EV discipline hasn't gone anywhere.</p>

<p>Yao's trader framing does real conceptual work — treating a sportsbook's line the way a market maker treats a bid-ask spread reframes 'finding value' from a gut-feel judgment about a team into a specific, checkable comparison between your own probability estimate and the implied probability baked into the odds, which is the same discipline that separates a professional trader from someone guessing.</p>

<p><strong>Key ideas:</strong></p>

<ul><li>Yao treats sportsbooks the way a trader treats a market — expected value is the one concept that actually matters, with everything else in service of estimating it.</li><li>Finding value in a betting line is the same skill as spotting a mispriced asset — it's a comparison, not a hunch.</li><li>Sharp bettors are framed explicitly as "traders" exploiting pricing inefficiencies, with conservative bankroll rules of 1–2% per wager.</li><li>Published in 2007, some market-structure advice is dated relative to today's more efficient, algorithmically-priced markets, though the EV discipline hasn't gone anywhere.</li></ul>
